Futures Basics

Options traders live next door to futures: the same underlyings, far more leverage, and no premium to collect — just pure directional exposure with daily cash settlement. This page takes you from "what even is a futures contract" to reading a specs table like a local, with honest math throughout.

What a Futures Contract Is

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell a specific quantity of something — an index, crude oil, gold — at a fixed price, on a fixed future date. Unlike an option, there is no right without obligation: both sides are obligated, and gains and losses settle in cash every single day.

Long (buy the future)

You agree to buy at the contract price. You profit if the price rises, lose dollar-for-dollar if it falls. One ES contract at 6,000 controls $300,000 of S&P 500 — every 1-point move is $50 in or out of your account.

Short (sell the future)

You agree to sell at the contract price. You profit if the price falls, lose dollar-for-dollar if it rises. No borrow, no locate, no "hard to borrow" list — shorting a future is as easy as selling one.

Key detail: almost nobody takes delivery. Before expiration, traders offset — sell what they bought, buy back what they sold — and the position simply disappears, leaving only the cash P&L. Futures are a trading vehicle, not a shopping list.

Margin & Leverage: The Double-Edged Part

Futures margin is a performance bond, not a down payment. You post a fraction of the contract's value as collateral, and you control the entire notional amount. That is where the leverage — and the danger — comes from.

The leverage math, honestly

Take one E-mini S&P (ES) contract with the index at 6,000:

$50 × 6,000 = $300,000 of notional exposure

Typical initial margin: roughly $13,000. So you control $300,000 with $13,000 — about 23-to-1 leverage.

A 1% move in the S&P — an ordinary day — is $3,000. That's ~23% of your margin, up or down. A 4–5% down week, the kind that happens a few times a year, wipes out the entire margin deposit and then some. This is not a flaw in futures; it is futures. Size accordingly.

Initial margin

What you post to open the position. Set by the exchange, raised when volatility rises. Think of it as the table stakes.

Maintenance margin

The lower line — usually ~90% of initial. If daily settlement pushes your equity below it, you get a margin call: deposit funds immediately or the broker liquidates you. There is no "wait and hope" setting.

The honest warning: leverage is symmetric — it multiplies gains and losses identically. Beginners should start with micro contracts (MES, MNQ: one-tenth the size of ES/NQ) or paper trade. A full-size NQ contract moves $20 per point; a 200-point Nasdaq swing — a normal morning — is $4,000.

Expiration, First Notice & the Roll

Futures don't last forever. Each contract has an expiration month, and traders who want to stay positioned must roll — close the expiring contract and open the next one.

Quarterly cycle

Equity index futures expire quarterly: March (H), June (M), September (U), December (Z). Energy and metals have monthly expirations. The nearest expiration is the front month — where almost all the volume lives.

First notice day

For physical commodities, this is the first day you could be assigned delivery. Long before that, liquidity migrates to the next contract. Practical rule: roll a week or two before expiration, never hold into delivery unless you own a refinery.

The cost of rolling

Rolling isn't free: you pay the spread twice (once to close, once to open), and if the curve is in contango, the next contract costs more than the one you're leaving — a small, repeated drag. See below.

Ticks & Contract Specs

Every contract defines a tick — the smallest allowed price move — and a point value: what one full point of movement is worth. Multiply them and you know exactly what each wiggle costs you.

Reading a specs table

Example — crude oil (CL): contract size 1,000 barrels, tick $0.01, tick value $10. If CL moves from $99.04 to $99.05, that's one tick = $10 per contract. A $1.00 move = 100 ticks = $1,000. The math is always: ticks × tick value = P&L.

Contango & Backwardation

Line up the same contract's expirations by date and you get the futures curve. Its shape tells you what the market expects — and it quietly taxes anyone who holds positions across rolls.

Contango — upward slope

Farther-dated contracts cost more than near ones. Normal for commodities with storage costs (crude, natural gas): the market charges you for carrying. Rolling a long position in contango means selling low, buying high each roll — a steady bleed. In 2020, crude's curve went so steeply into contango that the front month briefly printed negative.

Backwardation — downward slope

Farther-dated contracts cost less than near ones. Signals tight current supply — buyers pay up for barrels today. Rolling a long in backwardation means selling high and buying low: the curve pays you to hold. Equity index futures sit in mild contango most of the time (the cost of financing the position).

Why an options trader should care: the futures curve is the market's voted forecast of carry, not of direction — but it sets the financing assumptions baked into option prices. And if you ever hedge an options book with futures, rolling that hedge through a steep contango is a real, recurring cost.

The Contracts on This Site's Radar

These are the futures behind the market commentary here — the same ones on the homepage ticker.

ES — E-mini S&P 500

The world's most traded equity future. The benchmark for "the market." Nearly 24-hour trading, deep liquidity, tight spreads. If you trade one future, it's this one.

NQ — E-mini Nasdaq-100

Tech-heavy and twitchy: roughly 3–4× the point volatility of ES in dollar terms per contract. Bigger moves, bigger margin, bigger mistakes available.

YM — E-mini Dow

The Dow future. Smaller notional than ES, straightforward $5-per-point math. A calmer on-ramp to index futures than NQ.

CL — WTI crude

1,000 barrels of West Texas Intermediate per contract. Geopolitics in a ticker symbol: inventory reports Wednesdays, OPEC headlines anytime. $1 move = $1,000.

GC / SI — Gold & Silver

The classic fear gauges. Gold (100 oz/contract) trends; silver (5,000 oz) lurches — silver's percentage volatility routinely doubles gold's. Size silver positions smaller than the specs suggest.

HG — Copper

"Dr. Copper" — the metal with a PhD in economics. Tightly linked to growth expectations and electrification demand; 25,000 lbs per contract.

Key Risks for Beginners

Read this twice before your first trade.

Leverage cuts both ways

23-to-1 leverage means a 4–5% adverse move — routine a few times a year — erases the entire margin deposit. Futures have no "the premium was my max loss" safety rail. Your max loss is theoretically unbounded on a short.

Overnight gaps

Futures trade nearly 24 hours, but you sleep. Weekend news, Asian-session shocks, and inventory reports can gap prices far past your stop. Stops become market orders in a gap — your fill is wherever the market reopens.

Daily settlement is real money

Losses are debited every day, not at expiration. A position that's "fine if it comes back" still drains your account nightly and can trigger a margin call mid-drawdown.

Contract mix-ups

Trading the wrong expiration, the wrong size (full vs micro), or holding into first notice day are classic beginner errors. Confirm the symbol, month, and size on every order ticket.

The beginner's rule of thumb: risk only money you can afford to lose completely, start with micros, keep any single position small enough that a limit-move day doesn't end your account, and paper-trade the mechanics — especially rolling — before committing real capital.
